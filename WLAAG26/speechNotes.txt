SLIDE E.GIS 

The GIS Team’s work is almost entirely encapsulated within our Enterprise GIS system, but ‘what is an Enterprise GIS?’ 
 
At a basic level, it’s the central databases that make an ‘Enterprise’.  By leveraging SQL, or similar database software, you can safely allow multiple users to edit the same tables simultaneously.  The database even flags editing conflicts and helps managing user privileges – like who can edit the data versus who can read the data. 

But really, there is more than just the databases – in fact, most users never have to look at the database, because they can read and write to the database through our GIS-web servers.  We currently have 5 dedicated GIS servers... 

But we also have custom web pages and web applications, so we also have a dedicated web server, and an application-server for a vendor-supported asset management tool. 

Oh, and we also leverage ArcGIS.com -- a cloud environment.  Currently, this is useful for public surveys, but mission-critical for Emergency Management Planning: we can’t be reliant on our local power and network in the event of a disaster. 

SLIDE Permissions 

But don't be fooled by the sales pitch. Enterprise systems have their own challenges.  Securing your valuable data is a two-way street, with managing user-permissions and user administration being a big part of the day-to-day.  And once you have a multi-user environment up and running, you can’t just add a new field to a table... 

 

SLIDE Administration 

Of course, the positive spin is: an Enterprise provides the tools to manage those users in the first place.  You can fine tune editing privileges to only allow insert and update, you can implement data versioning and automate replications, you can track individual users’ activities, and ultimately, provide a stable web presence for your organization.

SLIDE CIP  

Sometimes I find it easier to demonstrate: one of my first projects for the City of this Project Tracking map.  Before we built this tool, projects were added to the GIS system from the ‘bid schedule’: this meant projects weren’t added to the map until their designs were 90%, and it meant a GIS Specialist had to guess where the project was based on a 255-character title. 

SLIDE CIP Editor 

So for this project, the big step forward was simple providing the City’s Engineers with an interface for editing their own projects.   This meant projects were being added to the GIS system early in their design proposals, and subject matter experts were locating the projects on the map.   
 
It was this simple editing interface that provided the City the confidence to make a public-facing splash page out of the GIS data. 

SLIDE CIP Collaboration 

But for me, building the Editor wasn’t the challenge.  The real challenge was getting everyone in the same room together: which fields are required?  How many different ‘Project Statuses’ are there?  What about ‘projects’ with multiple locations, like bids for sidewalk spot repairs or citywide tree removals? 
 
While getting all the various team members together was part of the challenge, it was also critical to the success of the project.

SLIDE Street Inspector 

I’ll be quick with this next project: I really can’t share many details anyway...  our Police Department handles parking concerns across the city, and they are really quite generous, usually giving several days of warnings before escalating to a civil citation and fine. 

SLIDE Street Ins. Workflow 

In the previous iteration of this application, it was possible for ‘Inspection records’ to get go missing because of an empty field, or an incorrectly set status. 

So the first thing we did when we sat down with PD was to discuss the actual workflow; then, once we understood each step, we clarified: what data can we auto-fill and what data needs to be user-editable. 

In the end, we built a custom form for each step.  The forms autofill most of the data the Officers needed, while still adding a space for ‘comments’ related to each step. 

I like sharing this project because it really highlights one of the critical skills I’ve seen across the IT department: whether we’re trying to automate a process, or just trying to ensure data validity in a largely human-centric process, the requirement is always the same: you have to understand the workflow in order to build a system around it.

SLIDE InfraView 

If you haven’t tried one of my QR codes yet, this would be a good one!  This one is a massive project, and it’s still in development – I'm sharing our internal beta test today! 

Many cities have a one stop “GIS Portal”, and the City of Cedar Rapids is no exception.  The City originally build the first Infrastructure Viewer in Web App Builder, a platform which is now deprecated and not long for this world.   

And I’m sharing this project, not because of its complexity or the importance to the city, but because of how it came about: it started with a request for a few simple tools that we just couldn’t provide out of the box. So, we started looking at the Javascript SDK. 

After I had shipped one tool, it became its own challenge to unpack everything in the SDK, and then to start stringing things together in useful ways... and before long, I had a template for modernizing our Infrastructure Viewer. 

No one told me to start this project – it didn't even start as a project.  It was – honestly – a way to stay busy at work during downtime.

So what do I hope you take from this talk? 

That if you’re interested in a professional GIS role, you’ll probably encounter an Enterprise system at some point in your career.  That these systems have their own unique challenges, but can that an Enterprise can also streamline data management.
